# Efficient Inference and Small Language Models: A Research Survey

## TL;DR
- Small-model capability is not just a parameter-count problem: data refinement, targeted curricula, and distillation are complementary levers, but published gains are task- and evaluation-specific.[1][2][3]
- Compression can reduce model footprint, yet sparsity or lower precision does not automatically yield lower end-to-end latency; acceleration depends on kernels, hardware, runtime, and workload.[4][5][6]
- Serving efficiency is strongly shaped by KV-cache allocation and scheduling: PagedAttention addresses fragmented cache memory, while iteration-level scheduling lets requests enter and leave active batches more flexibly.[7][8]
- Speculative decoding can reduce generation latency, but draft overhead and changing load conditions can erase or reverse gains; adaptive, workload-aware policies are a central direction.[9][10][11]

## Background
Small language models (SLMs) are compact language models intended to deliver useful capabilities under tighter compute, memory, latency, or deployment constraints than larger models. Efficient inference is broader than shrinking the parameter count: it includes the model representation, the sequence-generation algorithm, memory management, batch scheduling, and the hardware/software stack. The relevant objective is usually a constrained tradeoff among quality, time-to-first-token, per-token latency, throughput, memory use, and operating cost—not a single universal “efficiency” score.

Distillation provides an early, clear example of the quality/size tradeoff. DistilBERT applies distillation during pretraining and reports a model 40% smaller than BERT while retaining 97% of its language-understanding capabilities and running 60% faster in its evaluated settings.[12] Those results establish a useful compression precedent, but they concern an encoder and specific language-understanding tests; they should not be projected directly onto generative decoder models or different hardware. The later literature surveyed here extends the optimization target from the model itself to the data used to train it and to the full inference-serving path.

## 1. Building capable small models: data, distillation, and specialization
Three complementary strategies recur: curate better training examples, transfer knowledge from a stronger teacher, and train compact architectures with curricula targeted to desired capabilities. ProX makes data refinement a learnable, per-example programming process; its paper reports that models as small as 0.3B parameters can perform such refinement, and that ProX-curated data improved downstream benchmark results by more than 2% over original-data or data-selection baselines across C4, RedPajama-V2, and FineWeb.[1] This is evidence that data quality can improve the value extracted from a fixed training budget, although those comparisons are not a single general-purpose SLM leaderboard result.

Distillation methods differ in how they select examples and match the teacher. MiniLLM replaces the usual forward-KL objective with reverse-KL distillation for generative models and reports quality and calibration benefits across models from 120M to 13B parameters, though the retrieved evidence does not provide a numerical benchmark delta.[2] LLKD uses teacher confidence together with student information need to select unlabeled examples, but its abstract likewise gives qualitative rather than numerical evidence.[13] Task-specific “impossible distillation” reports a 770M model outperforming GPT-3 175B on summarization and paraphrasing, based on automatic and human evaluation; that unusually large comparison is restricted to those tasks and should not be interpreted as general capability parity.[14]

A newer from-scratch route is represented by Youtu-LLM, a 1.96B-parameter model trained on an approximately 11T-token corpus with a staged curriculum that shifts toward STEM and agentic tasks. The reported architecture includes compact dense Multi-Latent Attention and a 128k context window; however, the available abstract does not supply benchmark scores or inference speed/memory comparisons, so its claims of strong sub-2B performance cannot here be translated into quantified efficiency gains.[3] Taken together, the evidence favors designing around a target task and data regime rather than treating smallness as an architecture-only property. It also shows why quality evidence and serving-efficiency evidence must be reported separately.

## 2. Compression: pruning and quantization need systems support
Pruning and quantization can reduce representational or memory costs, but the operational benefit depends on whether the hardware and runtime can exploit the compressed format. SparseGPT reports one-shot pruning of GPT-family models to at least 50% sparsity without retraining and reports negligible perplexity increase at 60% unstructured sparsity in its tested large models.[4] That establishes a compression result, not a guarantee of proportional wall-clock acceleration: unstructured sparsity can be difficult to exploit efficiently, and the paper’s reported sparsity and quality figures are not universal latency measurements.

End-to-end CPU work illustrates the role of co-design. Fast DistilBERT combines hardware-aware structured pruning, distillation, INT8 post-training quantization, and sparse/quantized inference kernels; its source reports minimal SQuADv1.1 accuracy loss, performance up to 50% higher than DeepSparse, and up to 4.1× higher than ONNX Runtime.[5] The result is tied to DistilBERT and the evaluated CPU/runtime setup; it shows that a compressed model paired with optimized kernels matters more than nominal bit width alone.

Low-bit serving systems make this caveat especially visible. Atom reports up to 7.73× end-to-end throughput over FP16 and 2.53× over INT8 for its 4-bit weight-activation quantization at the same latency target, with the source describing accuracy loss as negligible.[15] QServe couples W4A8 weights/activations and a 4-bit KV cache with system optimizations; on its tested setups it reports throughput gains over TensorRT-LLM ranging from 1.2× to 3.5× depending on model and GPU.[6] Crucially, QServe notes that existing INT4 methods can incur 20–90% runtime overhead from dequantization or partial-sum handling. These papers therefore support a conditional conclusion: quantization is a capacity and compute opportunity, but usable speedup depends on low-overhead kernels, calibration/accuracy control, and the exact inference stack.[5][6]

## 3. KV-cache memory and serving schedules
Autoregressive serving stores key/value states for active sequences, so cache allocation can constrain how many requests fit on a device. PagedAttention organizes each sequence’s KV cache into fixed-size, non-contiguous blocks, allocated as needed and shareable across requests; the vLLM paper reports 2–4× throughput over FasterTransformer and Orca at the same latency in its evaluated workloads, with larger gains for longer sequences, larger models, and complex decoding.[7] The main transferable insight is the mechanism—reduce fragmentation and enable flexible sharing—not the benchmark multiplier, which depends on workload and hardware.

Memory representation can also be compressed directly. KIVI uses asymmetric 2-bit quantization, with keys quantized per-channel and values per-token; its paper reports nearly maintained quality while reducing peak memory by 2.6×, enabling up to 4× larger batch size and 2.35×–3.47× throughput on its real-inference workloads.[16] That benefit is paired with implementation costs such as quantization during prefill/decode and a full-precision residual cache, so cache compression should be evaluated end-to-end rather than as a memory-only metric.

Scheduling determines how effectively available memory and compute are used. Orca’s iteration-level scheduling executes one generation iteration at a time, allowing completed requests to exit and new arrivals to enter without waiting for an entire batch to finish; its source reports 36.9× throughput improvement over FasterTransformer at matched latency for the evaluated GPT-3 175B setup.[8] This is a workload-specific result, but the scheduling principle complements PagedAttention: the first improves request-level flexibility, the second cache placement and sharing. In practice, maximum batch size is not automatically optimal because long prefills can delay decode iterations, and latency targets constrain admission and scheduling choices.[7][8]

## 4. Speculative decoding: acceleration depends on the regime
Speculative decoding uses a cheaper process to draft candidate tokens and a target model to verify them, aiming to reduce expensive sequential target-model steps. EAGLE predicts using intermediate model features and reports 2.7×–3.5× latency speedup for LLaMA2-Chat 70B.[9] The result is tied to that model and evaluated decoding setup, not an invariant property of speculation. The EAGLE source also gives a counterexample: a 7B draft model used with a 13B target can be less efficient than vanilla autoregressive decoding because drafting overhead can outweigh saved target steps.[9]

The batch-size story is similarly conditional. MagicDec argues that speculative decoding can help high-throughput inference on moderate-to-long sequences, challenging the assumption that it only helps at small batch sizes, but the retrieved abstract does not provide numerical speedups sufficient for direct comparison.[17] Nightjar frames the policy problem dynamically: speculation may help low-load, memory-bound serving but hurt high-load, compute-bound serving because verification overhead persists; it proposes adapting speculation to workload rather than relying on fixed draft lengths.[10] Meta’s large-scale Llama work likewise reports model-dependent trends: speedup increased with batch size in one Llama 3.1 8B result but decreased with batch size for Llama 4 Maverick, and the source identifies added FLOP demand and resource-management risks at larger batches.[11] The cross-paper implication is that draft length, acceptance, target/draft cost, batch size, and bottleneck must be measured together.

## Trends and open problems
The literature is moving from isolated model compression toward co-design across data, model, and serving. Data refinement and task-focused curricula attempt to raise capability per training token; distillation offers another path, while parameter-efficient fine-tuning results caution that more aggressive parameter reduction is not uniformly beneficial: an HF-daily study reports that several structural PEFT variants outperform standard LoRA in its RLVR setting, while very aggressive reductions such as VeRA and Rank-1 bottleneck reasoning capacity.[18] This evidence is specific to fine-tuning distilled reasoning models, but reinforces the need to measure capability after compression or adaptation rather than infer it from parameter counts.

Several evaluation gaps remain. First, comparisons need a common end-to-end reporting frame: quality at a defined task mix, time-to-first-token, inter-token latency, throughput at stated latency/SLO, memory footprint, model and runtime versions, hardware, batch/load profile, and energy or cost where available. Many headline gains in these notes are maxima from particular settings, and some abstracts omit enough detail to prevent apples-to-apples comparison.[3][15][17] Second, systems gains can interact: quantization changes kernel costs and cache capacity; cache capacity changes batching; batching changes whether speculative decoding helps. Optimizing each component independently can therefore misstate the end-to-end result.[6][16][10]

Third, quality retention is multidimensional. Task-specific distillation wins do not establish broad equivalence to a large teacher, and benchmark averages may hide failure on long contexts, tool use, or domain shifts.[14][3] Fourth, adaptive serving needs policies that respond to request length, concurrency, acceptance rate, and hardware bottlenecks without imposing measurement or restart overhead that cancels gains.[10][11] A useful next generation of studies would publish reproducible traces and compare systems at matched quality and service-level objectives, including both low-load interactive use and high-throughput serving. The most defensible general conclusion is not that one compression or decoding method always wins, but that efficient inference is an end-to-end, workload-conditioned design problem.
