# Video and Multimodal Generation: Architectures, Control, and Evaluation

## TL;DR
- Video generation has moved from compressed-latent autoregressive and masked-token approaches toward a diverse design space that also includes cascaded diffusion; these are complementary choices that trade sequence structure, flexible generation, and denoising cost rather than a single settled winner. [1][2][3][4][5]
- Recent systems increasingly combine scale and task breadth: Movie Gen describes 1080p video with synchronized audio and a 30B-parameter video model, while HunyuanVideo reports a 13B-plus open model; these are model-paper specifications and claims, not directly comparable independent rankings. [6][7]
- Multimodal generation is not only “text in, video out”: systems use reference images, structured motion/scene controls, and audio tracks, but one conditioning pathway rarely solves all control needs. [8][9][10]
- Evaluation is broadening beyond frame aesthetics and prompt matching toward physical faithfulness, event causality, world consistency, and cross-modal alignment; benchmarks diagnose different failure modes, and their scores are not interchangeable. [11][12][13][14][15]

## Background
Video generation maps one or more conditions—such as text, an image, audio, or a prior video—to a temporally ordered visual sequence, sometimes with synchronized sound. Unlike a still image, a video must preserve appearance and scene structure across time while making motion and events plausible. A diffusion-focused survey lists text, images, video, audio, noise, and 3D point clouds among possible conditions, while emphasizing motion consistency, long-video generation, compute cost, and physical plausibility as persistent difficulties. [16]

Foundational approaches explored adversarial generators and disentangled motion/content representations; subsequent work made compressed visual tokens a practical interface for sequence models. VideoGPT uses a 3D VQ-VAE to compress video and an autoregressive transformer to predict discrete latents, reducing the sequence burden relative to pixel-level generation. [1][16] Diffusion work instead adapts denoising to spatiotemporal data, including joint frame generation and conditional rollouts for longer sequences. [2] These approaches establish the central design tension of the field: how to represent video compactly, model cross-frame dependencies, and generate long or detailed outputs within tractable compute.

## Generative architectures and temporal structure
Token-based approaches turn video into a sequence or set of discrete codes. VideoGPT predicts codes autoregressively, whereas Phenaki uses a causal video tokenizer and bidirectional masked transformer to support variable-length video and sequences of time-varying text prompts. MAGVIT also uses spatiotemporal tokens but trains with masked-token modeling and multi-task learning, distinguishing its objective from left-to-right next-token generation. [1][3][5] Their shared advantage is a reusable compressed representation; their design differences affect how temporal order and variable duration are handled. Tokenizer quality and sequence length matter jointly: MAGVIT notes that tokenization sets both reconstruction quality and generation efficiency. [5]

Diffusion systems take a different route by iteratively denoising spatiotemporal representations. Video Diffusion Models extend image diffusion with a 3D U-Net and combine joint generation of a fixed frame block with autoregressive conditional sampling for longer videos. [2] Imagen Video uses a cascade of base generation and spatial/temporal super-resolution stages; its space-time-separable U-Net interleaves temporal and spatial operations, assigning long-range temporal attention to the base model and local temporal convolution to upsampling stages. [4] In practice, this makes “diffusion versus autoregression” an incomplete dichotomy: denoising can operate inside a larger autoregressive rollout, and cascades allocate resolution and temporal work across stages. [2][4]

Claims of efficiency need their experimental context. MAGVIT reports inference two orders of magnitude faster than the diffusion models and 60 times faster than the autoregressive models in its own compared setting, but those ratios should not be generalized across architectures or workloads. [5] Diffusion papers, meanwhile, identify computation and sampling as central burdens and use architectural staging or progressive distillation to manage them. [2][4] Thus, latency, output duration/resolution, hardware, and quality should be assessed together rather than inferred from a family label.

## Scaling and increasingly general video systems
Recent work frames video generators as broad media systems, not merely short text-to-video samplers. HunyuanVideo describes an open-source model with more than 13 billion parameters and attributes its framework to data curation, architecture, progressive scaling/training, and training/inference infrastructure. [6] Movie Gen reports a family of media models, including a 30B-parameter video model, 1080p generation with synchronized audio, and a maximum 73K-video-token context corresponding to 16 seconds at 16 frames per second. [7] These specifications illustrate attempts to scale duration, resolution, and modalities together, but the cited descriptions do not provide a common controlled comparison between the systems.

The open-model scaling trade-off remains explicit. HunyuanVideo 1.5 presents an 8.3B-parameter text- and image-to-video model and claims strong quality and motion coherence among open models; its report emphasizes attention design, progressive training, and super-resolution as part of its efficiency strategy. [17] These are author-reported claims. Differences in parameter count alone do not establish quality, cost, or generality: data, sampling, conditioning, and evaluation protocols also differ, and the supplied source summaries do not make all of those factors commensurable. [17][6][7]

## Multimodal conditioning, control, and audio-video generation
Control ranges from high-level text to explicit spatial and temporal signals. A controllable-generation survey groups conditions into structural controls (such as pose, depth, sketches, and boxes), identity/reference inputs, temporal controls (including trajectories, camera, and motion), audio, and multi-condition approaches. It argues that text alone can underspecify fine-grained requirements and that adapting new controls to pretrained text-to-video models remains challenging. [8] This taxonomy is useful precisely because it separates distinct user intentions; it does not imply one generator reliably supports every combination.

For audio, the conditioning direction and task definition matter. ReWaS uses video to estimate sound structure and text to specify key content, allowing separate adjustment of energy, environment, and primary sound source; it targets controllable sound generation rather than generating or editing the video itself. [9] MMAudio generates audio conditioned on video and optional text, and adds a frame-level synchronization module to align video conditions with audio latents. It focuses on Foley-style sound effects and explicitly does not target background music or speech. [18] These examples show that “multimodal” can mean either using one modality to generate another or coordinating multiple outputs, and those task settings should not be conflated.

Joint audiovisual generation makes timing a first-class control problem. MTV separates speech, sound-effect, and music tracks to control lip motion, event timing, and visual mood, respectively. [10][19] This disentanglement addresses ambiguity in mapping a single audio representation to visual events, although the reported metric leadership is the authors’ claim and the evidence does not establish arbitrary-control robustness. [19] T2AV-Compass evaluates text-to-audio-video generation jointly, using 500 prompts and objective signal-level metrics alongside judge-based assessments; its authors report persistent problems in realism, fine-grained synchronization, and instruction following across the 15 systems evaluated. [15]

Editing presents a further alignment/preservation trade-off. One sequential approach edits video first and then conditions audio generation on source audio, edited video, and text; it adapts source-audio retention to edit complexity in order to balance instruction fidelity, alignment, and preservation. The authors report better alignment and content integrity, but identify object addition/deletion as future work. [20] JoVA instead proposes joint video-audio generation and editing with shared multimodal interactions and reference conditioning; it reports lip-sync results but notes limitations in fast hand motion, text-rich scenes, and general-scene paired training data. [21] Together these approaches expose a core choice: sequential pipelines can reuse specialized video editors but must repair cross-modal mismatch afterward, whereas joint systems can model modalities together but face data and control challenges.

## Evaluation: from appearance to world and cross-modal consistency
Video evaluation is multidimensional. VBench++ is designed around fine-grained dimensions, tailored prompts/methods, and human-preference annotations to expose different strengths and weaknesses rather than collapse quality into one number. [11] VBench-2.0 argues that aesthetics, temporal consistency, and basic prompt adherence can miss intrinsic faithfulness; it evaluates human fidelity, controllability, creativity, physics, and commonsense using generalist and specialized evaluators. [12] The shift is from “does this look plausible?” toward “does it obey physical, anatomical, and commonsense constraints?” [12]

Event-level measures probe another weakness. VideoVerse argues that frame aesthetics and temporal consistency are not enough to test temporal causality and world knowledge; its benchmark uses 300 prompts, 815 events, and 793 questions across ten dimensions, and reports a gap between evaluated generators and desired world-model capability. [13] The World Consistency Score proposes object permanence, relation stability, causal compliance, and flicker penalty as interpretable components, motivated by the inability of distribution-level and semantic metrics to identify logical errors inside an individual clip. However, the retrieved paper describes validation as a planned experiment rather than established performance, so WCS should be treated as a proposal, not a proven replacement metric. [14]

For audio-video systems, unimodal quality is insufficient: generated sound can be high quality yet mistimed or semantically mismatched. T2AV-Compass explicitly combines video, audio, and cross-modal metrics with judge-based diagnostics, and reports synchronization and instruction-following failures in evaluated systems. [15] Benchmark results remain protocol-dependent: annotation choices, model judges, prompt sets, and task scope shape what a score means. The evidence here supports complementary evaluation axes, not a single universal leaderboard. [11][12][13][15]

## Trends and open problems
- **Longer, more coherent generation:** Tokenized and diffusion-based approaches offer different mechanisms for duration and temporal dependency, but the sources continue to identify long-video consistency and physical plausibility as unresolved. [2][3][16]
- **Scalable but controllable models:** Recent model reports emphasize model/data scale and infrastructure, while control work emphasizes conditions beyond text. A key research question is how to add spatial, temporal, identity, and audio controls without losing coherence or making inference prohibitively expensive. [17][6][7][8]
- **Reliable audiovisual alignment:** Track-specific conditioning and joint generation provide mechanisms for tighter coordination, yet benchmarks and system reports still identify timing, realism, and instruction-following failures. [18][15][10][19]
- **Better evaluation and evidence:** Fine-grained human-aligned and intrinsic-faithfulness benchmarks expand the target beyond aesthetics, but metrics are task- and protocol-specific; proposed world-consistency measures need empirical validation, and author-reported comparisons should not be mistaken for independent rankings. [11][12][13][14]
- **Data and generalization:** The surveyed sources identify high-quality video-text data, compute, and paired general-scene audiovisual data as constraints. Improving coverage of scenes, motion, causality, and control combinations remains necessary before results on curated tasks can support claims of broad capability. [3][16][21]
