# World Models in AI: Representations, Prediction, Planning, and Evaluation

## TL;DR
- A world model is best treated as a family of learned predictive representations used to anticipate action-conditioned futures, not as one settled architecture or necessarily a photorealistic simulator; definitions span predictive state, latent dynamics, and future simulation for decision-making. [1][2]
- The field has moved from compact latent dynamics and model-predictive control toward task-oriented hidden-state planning, imagined-trajectory policy learning, and scalable video foundation models; these design families optimize different notions of “useful” prediction. [3][4][5][6]
- Visual realism is not enough for an agent: newer evaluations test video-to-action consistency, closed-loop control, policy-ranking value, and long-horizon causal reasoning. [7][8][9][10]
- There is promising but bounded evidence for policy evaluation in learned worlds: WorldGym reports that simulated policy success correlates with real-world success, while also noting persistent interaction errors. [8]
- The central open problem is dependable action-conditioned prediction under distribution shift and over long horizons, plus evaluations that establish transfer to real tasks rather than only plausible generated videos or benchmark performance. [3][7][8]

## Background

“World model” has no universally settled definition. In a narrow model-based reinforcement-learning (MBRL) sense, it is a learned environment model—often state-transition and reward predictions—used to plan or improve a policy. In broader usage it includes internal representations that predict future observations, states, or outcomes, whether or not they reconstruct the whole environment. A recent survey explicitly distinguishes understanding or representing the world from predicting future states for simulation and decision-making. [2]

The conceptual roots predate the contemporary neural terminology. Predictive state representations define state through predictions of future action-observation sequences, rather than requiring a hidden-state belief or a reconstructive model; the work also leaves practical learnability as an open question. [1] Ha and Schmidhuber’s 2018 “World Models” popularized a compact neural formulation: an encoder compresses observations, a recurrent model predicts future latent codes probabilistically, and a controller acts on these representations. Their framework motivates learning and acting inside an imagined environment, conditional on the model being accurate enough for the task. [11]

A useful distinction is therefore between the *representation* (what information is retained), the *predictor* (what future is forecast), and the *use* (planning, policy learning, evaluation, or generation). Later systems make different trade-offs among these components: some aim to predict observations and rewards, some learn only planning-relevant hidden quantities, and some generate controllable video. These alternatives should not be conflated with a single general-purpose simulator. [4][5][2]

## Theme 1: Latent dynamics and planning-oriented models

A foundational practical strategy is to compress high-dimensional visual input into a latent state and plan over predicted latent transitions. PlaNet learns a latent dynamics model from interaction, including transition, observation, and reward components, and uses online model-predictive control (MPC) to repeatedly plan candidate action sequences and replan as observations arrive. Latent overshooting trains predictions over multiple steps. The paper reports using substantially fewer episodes while reaching final performance close to, and sometimes higher than, strong model-free algorithms; this comparison is bounded to the paper’s evaluation setup rather than a universal sample-efficiency guarantee. [12][3]

MuZero illustrates a complementary, task-oriented definition of a world model. Instead of reconstructing every observation or encoding all physical rules, it learns hidden-state dynamics that predict reward, value, and policy for search. It combines this model with tree search and reports evaluation on 57 Atari games as well as superhuman performance in Go, chess, and shogi without being given those games’ dynamics. The model is “world-like” insofar as it preserves information needed by the planner, not because its latent state must be human-interpretable or visually complete. [4]

DreamerV3 shifts emphasis from online action-sequence search toward learning a policy and value function from imagined latent trajectories. Its world model supplies predicted outcomes; actor and critic components learn from those imagined sequences, with experience replay supporting training. The paper reports outperforming specialized methods across more than 150 diverse tasks, including collecting Minecraft diamonds from scratch without human data or curricula. This breadth is notable, but it does not establish arbitrary real-world transfer or general-purpose world understanding. [5]

Together these systems clarify a persistent engineering choice: an explicit planner can optimize candidate actions against a learned model, while an imagination-trained actor can amortize decisions into a policy. Both depend on the model being useful over the trajectories the agent considers. PlaNet’s authors identify inaccurate models, compounding multi-step prediction error, failure to represent multiple futures, and overconfident extrapolation outside training data as core difficulties. [3][5]

## Theme 2: Predictive representations versus generative video worlds

Not every useful world model needs to generate pixels. JEPA proposes predicting target representations from context representations, rather than generating all target details; the motivation is to focus prediction on abstract, predictable structure and to permit latent variables for multiple plausible outcomes. Its hierarchical proposal links short-term lower-level prediction with higher-level abstraction and longer-horizon planning. The cited work is a research agenda, not evidence that a complete hierarchical autonomous agent has been demonstrated. [13]

A different recent line treats video generation as an interactive environment. Genie combines a spatiotemporal video tokenizer, an autoregressive dynamics model, and a latent-action model; its reported 11-billion-parameter system learns controllable environments from unlabeled videos and supports frame-by-frame interaction without ground-truth action labels. This offers a way to infer action structure from video, but latent actions are not automatically equivalent to semantic instructions or a robot’s embodiment-specific control interface. [6]

Other video approaches vary in how action information enters the model. iVideoGPT tokenizes observations, actions, and rewards for autoregressive next-token prediction and is presented for visual planning and model-based RL; the retrieved abstract describes pretraining over millions of human and robotic manipulation trajectories, but reports only qualitative “competitive” performance. AVID instead adapts a pretrained image-to-video diffusion model using an adapter and mask, without requiring access to or fine-tuning of the pretrained parameters; it still requires domain-specific action-labelled videos. [14][15]

Foundation-model efforts emphasize reusable video priors and adaptation pipelines. Cosmos describes a platform spanning video curation, pretrained world foundation models, post-training data, and video tokenizers for Physical AI. Its abstract supports the platform approach but does not provide a quantitative downstream planning result. Navigation World Models use a 1-billion-parameter conditional diffusion transformer trained on egocentric videos and predict future observations conditioned on navigation actions; the paper reports visual-navigation results, but those results do not by themselves establish general-purpose manipulation or broad embodied competence. [16][17]

These approaches encode different assumptions. Latent predictive methods can omit visually irrelevant detail; generative models can provide rich imagined observations and flexible interfaces, but visual fidelity does not ensure causal or action fidelity. The appropriate architecture depends on what downstream decisions require, what action supervision is available, and whether the intended use is planning, policy learning, interaction, or data generation. [13][6][15][7]

## Theme 3: From generated futures to embodied action

An important distinction is whether a video model directly predicts an action-conditioned future for a particular agent, or instead supplies a visual target that another controller must interpret. Gen2Act uses language-conditioned human-video generation in novel scenarios followed by execution through a single robot policy conditioned on the generated video. The authors report real-world manipulation involving unseen object types and novel motions for tasks absent from robot training data; the paper describes using an order of magnitude less robot-interaction data than the video predictor’s training data, but its abstract provides no exact task-success rates. The setup therefore demonstrates a pipeline for transferring motion information, not a video generator that directly outputs low-level robot actions. [18]

Navigation World Models provide a closer action-conditioned loop: the model predicts observations from prior observations and navigation actions, and candidate imagined trajectories can be evaluated for goal achievement in familiar environments. The paper also reports generating imagined trajectories from a single image in unfamiliar environments; that ability should be distinguished from its familiar-environment planning evaluation. More generally, action-conditioned models need to preserve the details relevant to control, while avoiding actions or imagined states that exploit model errors. [17]

Evaluation proposals increasingly test this distinction directly. WorldSimBench combines perceptual assessment—including human preference—with manipulative evaluation of whether generated videos translate into correct control signals, across embodied environments, autonomous driving, and robot manipulation. Its motivation is that visual quality alone cannot establish video-action consistency; the retrieved abstract describes benchmark design rather than numerical comparative results. [7]

Recent work also explores hybrid representations and richer interaction coverage. MoWM motivates combining pixel-space diffusion with compressed latent features: pixel reconstructions may carry redundant visual content, while compressed latents may omit fine detail needed for manipulation. Two Hugging Face Daily Papers summaries similarly identify action-faithful prediction as an active concern: DreamTrue targets calibration and sparse unsuccessful interactions through rendered action trajectories and counterfactual post-training, while RoboJEPA describes a latent model spanning 12 robotic embodiments and frames principled capability scaling as open. The retrieved summaries provide no quantitative downstream success or scaling parameters, so they support research directions, not comparative effectiveness. [19][20][21]

## Theme 4: Evaluating whether a model is a useful world

Evaluation must separate at least four questions: Does the output look plausible? Does it predict the consequences of actions? Does it support long-horizon task reasoning? Does using it improve decisions or policy evaluation in a closed loop? Benchmarks measure different subsets of these questions, and their scores should not be treated as interchangeable. [7][9][10]

WorldGym tests the model as an environment for policy evaluation. The study reports a high correlation between policy success in the world model and real-world success, and says rollouts preserve relative rankings across policy versions, sizes, and checkpoints. It also says object interaction remains challenging and per-task simulated results differ from real outcomes. Correlation and ranking utility make the model a promising proxy in this evaluation—not a substitute for physical validation. [8]

World-in-World argues for closed-loop action APIs, online planning, and task success across four environments, rather than relying on open-loop visual quality. The paper reports that allocating more inference-time compute improved closed-loop performance and that scaling post-training with action-observation data was more effective than replacing the pretrained video generator. These findings describe the paper’s setup; they do not show that the same interventions improve every task or model. [9]

Video benchmarks also expose a gap between visual quality and whether a model captures action-relevant dynamics. WorldSimBench combines perceptual assessment, including human preference, with manipulative evaluation of whether generated videos translate into correct control signals. Its benchmark covers embodied environments, autonomous driving, and robot manipulation; the retrieved abstract describes the evaluation design but does not give numerical model comparisons. [7]

WorldPrediction probes higher-level action-state causality and long-horizon procedural planning, and reports a substantial gap between frontier models and human performance on its tasks. Its focus is human-centric activity and discriminative planning, so its results should not be generalized to robot control without further evidence. [10]

## Trends and open problems

- **Causal, action-faithful prediction:** Models must forecast what follows from interventions, not merely produce a plausible continuation. Benchmarks that assess action conversion and closed-loop task success address this gap, and the benchmark evidence points to distinct but complementary measures of actionability and long-horizon reasoning. [7][9][10]
- **Long-horizon reliability and uncertainty:** Prediction errors compound over rollout, and models may miss alternative futures or be overconfident outside their training distribution. Robust planning therefore requires uncertainty-aware prediction, recalibration from observations, and methods that keep selected actions within regions where the model has support. [3][15]
- **Representation and control alignment:** Latent states, pixels, inferred latent actions, and embodiment-specific action labels each preserve different information. JEPA-style prediction avoids reconstructing every detail; video-diffusion methods provide rich observations; hybrid approaches seek to retain task-relevant geometry and motion. The remaining question is how to measure whether a representation retains exactly what the downstream controller needs. [13][6][15][19][20]
- **Data efficiency and generalization:** Unlabeled video and reusable video priors can broaden visual coverage, but conditioning for control may still require action-labelled domain data or a separately trained policy. Cross-embodiment and counterfactual-data approaches are promising directions, yet the available summaries do not establish their comparative transfer performance. [6][15][16][18][20]
- **Evaluation beyond a single score:** Policy-ranking correlation, action consistency, and long-horizon planning accuracy measure distinct capabilities. A credible evaluation suite should combine open-loop diagnostics with closed-loop behavior and, where deployment is intended, real-world tests; current results remain bounded by their task suites and simulation fidelity. [7][8][9][10]
- **Scaling and scientific clarity:** Large video models and robotic datasets motivate questions about scaling with model, data, and compute, but the available RoboJEPA summary explicitly leaves principled scaling estimates open and supplies no quantitative coefficients. More generally, definitions and evaluation practices remain fragmented, making cross-paper comparisons difficult. [20][2]

Overall, world models are most productively understood as task-linked predictive systems: their value depends on the match between representation, action interface, rollout fidelity, and downstream use. The field’s progress is real—from latent control and planning to interactive video and model-based evaluation—but evidence for reliable, transferable prediction is still substantially narrower than the broad “world model” label may imply. [3][7][8][2]
